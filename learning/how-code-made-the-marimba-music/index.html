First: what is a marimba?

A marimba is a big wooden xylophone. It has a row of wooden bars. You hit the bars with soft mallets, like sticks with balls of yarn on the end.

Long bars make low notes. Short bars make high notes. Under each bar hangs a metal tube. The tube makes the note louder and warmer, like cupping your hands around your mouth.

Try it. Tap the bars.

Tap a bar. Try the white letters C, E, G one after the other.

Marimba music sounds warm, round and bouncy. Each note starts with a soft "tok" and then fades quickly. The instrument comes from Africa and Central America. Today it is the national instrument of Guatemala. You may know one marimba sound already: the first iPhone's default ringtone was called "Marimba".

Because each note is short and clear, marimba music is great for a fast video. The notes hit the beat and get out of the way.

The problem

The video changes picture almost every second. We want the music to "hit" each change: a ding when the light bulb pops, a beat when the new site slides in.

How do people usually get music?

  • Download a song. But the song does not know where our picture changes are. The beats land in the wrong places.
  • Play a real marimba and record it. But we have no marimba, no player and no microphone. Also, Claude cannot hear. It cannot listen and say "a bit softer, please".

Root causeWe need sound that we can place to the exact millisecond, using only code, without ears.

So how can we make a marimba sound with only a computer?

To answer that, we first need to know what a sound is.

What is sound?

Throw a stone in a pond. Rings of ripples move out. Sound is like that, but in the air. Something shakes, and it pushes the air in tiny waves. The waves reach your ear, and you hear them.

A speaker makes these waves with a thin cone that moves in and out. The computer tells the cone where to be. It does this 44,100 times each second. Each "where to be" is one number.

LikeA flip book. Each page is one number. Flip 44,100 pages each second and you get a sound.

Key ideaA sound is just a long list of numbers. If we can write the list, we can make any sound. We do not need an instrument.

What list of numbers makes a marimba?

First, a quick look at the tools. Then we build one, step by step.

The tools in the box

The program is one Python file, about 100 lines. It uses only a few common tools. There is no music library, no sound samples and no music app.

PythonThe language the program is written in.
NumPyDoes maths on long lists of numbers in one go. It makes the waves (np.sin), the fades (np.exp) and the noise (random.normal).
SciPyTwo small jobs. Its filters (scipy.signal) shape the noise into a clap, a shaker or a whoosh. scipy.io.wavfile saves the numbers as a sound file.
ffmpegA free command-line tool. It sets the final loudness and glues the sound to the video.

Every sound starts the same way: a list of times, one for each of the 44,100 numbers in a second.

Step 1: a pure tone

The simplest sound is a smooth wave that goes up and down, again and again, like a swing. How fast it swings is the pitch. 523 swings each second is the note C.

It sounds like a microwave oven's beep. Not like wood.

Step 1 · a pure tone
f = 523                                  # the note C: 523 swings each second
sound = np.sin(2 * np.pi * f * t)        # one smooth wave, the same from start to end

Why is a real marimba different from a beep?

Root causeA wooden bar makes many notes at the same time, not one. And its sound does not stay the same: it is loud at the hit and then it gets quiet.

Step 2: add overtones

When the mallet hits the bar, the bar wobbles in more than one way at once. It sings its main note, plus some higher, quieter notes. These are overtones.

LikeA smoothie. Mostly banana (the main note), a bit of strawberry, and a tiny bit of mint. The mix gives the taste. The mix of overtones gives the "wooden" taste of the sound.

Marimba makers carve an arch under each bar to tune these overtones. Our recipe adds one overtone about 4 times higher (at about a quarter of the loudness) and one about 9 times higher (very quiet). See the three ingredients in the picture.

Now it is brighter. But it never stops. It sounds like an organ.

Step 2 · add two overtones
sound = (      np.sin(2 * np.pi * f        * t)     # main note
       + 0.28 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * f * 3.93 * t)     # about 4x higher, quieter
       + 0.07 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * f * 9.2  * t))    # about 9x higher, very quiet

Step 3: add the fade

After you hit a bar, it rings and then it slowly goes quiet, like a bouncing ball that bounces lower and lower.

So we make the wave smaller and smaller over time. The high overtones fade faster than the main note, just like on a real bar. After a moment, you hear mostly the warm main note.

Step 3 · add the fade
# np.exp(-t * k) starts at 1 and slides towards 0. A bigger k means a faster fade.
sound = (      np.sin(2 * np.pi * f        * t) * np.exp(-t * 3.2)    # slow fade
       + 0.28 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * f * 3.93 * t) * np.exp(-t * 13)     # faster
       + 0.07 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * f * 9.2  * t) * np.exp(-t * 32))    # fastest

Step 4: add the mallet "tok"

A real hit starts with a tiny "tok": the mallet touching the wood. It is not a note. It is a very short noise, like a spoon tapping a table.

So we add 4 thousandths of a second of random numbers at the very start. Your ear hears it as the hit. Now it sounds like a marimba.

Step 4 · add the "tok"
tok = int(0.004 * SR)                                    # 4 ms = 176 numbers
noise = np.random.normal(0, 0.15, tok)                   # random numbers = noise
sound[:tok] += noise * np.linspace(1, 0, tok)            # loud at first, then gone
All four steps as one function (the real code) 
def marimba(note, length=1.2):
    t = np.arange(int(length * 44100)) / 44100      # the time of each number
    f = 440 * 2 ** ((note - 69) / 12)                # note number to pitch (Hz)
    s = (np.sin(2*np.pi*f*t)            * np.exp(-t*3.2)   # main note, slow fade
       + 0.28*np.sin(2*np.pi*f*3.93*t)  * np.exp(-t*13)    # small overtone, faster fade
       + 0.07*np.sin(2*np.pi*f*9.2*t)   * np.exp(-t*32))   # tiny overtone, fastest fade
    tok = int(0.004 * 44100)                         # 4 ms
    s[:tok] += np.random.normal(0, .15, tok) * np.linspace(1, 0, tok)
    return s

From one note to music

We can make one marimba note. How do we make a song?

A song is just the right notes at the right times. So the program needs two lists: which notes, and when.

  • Which note. Notes are like steps on a ladder. Each step up is about 6% higher. Twelve steps make the same note again, twice as high. The code turns a step number into a pitch.
  • When. A clock ticks 120 times each minute, so one tick is half a second. Every sound sits on a tick, or halfway between two ticks.
  • Which notes sound good together. Groups of notes that sound nice together are chords. The song loops four chords: C, G, A minor, F. Very many pop songs use these four, because they sound happy.
  • Drums, made the same way with numbers. A kick drum is a low tone that drops fast. A clap is three quick bursts of noise. A shaker is a very short "tss".

One bar, eight half-ticks. A filled box means "play here". The bar repeats with a new chord each time.

Python · which note
def hz(note):                       # a step on the ladder -> pitch
    return 440 * 2 ** ((note - 69) / 12)

hz(69)   # 440.0  (A)
hz(72)   # 523.3  (C)
hz(84)   # 1046.5 (the next C, twice as high)
Python · when (simplified)
song = np.zeros(16 * SR)                  # 16 seconds of silence to fill

def add(sound, at):                       # put a sound into the song at a time (seconds)
    i = int(at * SR)
    song[i:i + len(sound)] += sound[:len(song) - i]

BEAT = 0.5                                # 120 beats each minute
CHORDS = {3.5: [72, 76, 79, 76, 72, 67, 76, 79],    # C
          5.5: [71, 74, 79, 74, 71, 67, 74, 79],    # G
          7.5: [72, 76, 81, 76, 72, 69, 76, 81],    # A minor
          9.5: [69, 72, 77, 72, 69, 65, 72, 77]}    # F
for start, notes in CHORDS.items():
    for k, n in enumerate(notes):
        add(marimba(n), start + k * BEAT / 2)       # 8 notes, half a beat apart
Python · drums from numbers
def kick():                               # a low tone that drops fast in pitch
    t = np.arange(int(0.35 * SR)) / SR
    pitch = 48 + 110 * np.exp(-t * 38)    # 158 Hz falling to 48 Hz
    return np.sin(2 * np.pi * np.cumsum(pitch) / SR) * np.exp(-t * 9)

def shaker():                             # a very short, very high "tss"
    t = np.arange(int(0.06 * SR)) / SR
    tss = np.random.normal(0, 1, len(t)) * np.exp(-t * 70)

Mix it and save it

  1. Add everything up, one list for the left speaker and one for the right.
  2. Round off the loudest bits, so nothing crackles.
  3. Set the volume to a normal level for online video, and save it as a sound file.
  4. Glue it to the video with a tool called ffmpeg.
Python · mix and save
from scipy.io import wavfile

mix = np.tanh(song * 1.1) / np.tanh(1.1)          # round off the loudest bits
mix = mix * 0.89 / np.max(np.abs(mix))            # loudest point just under the top
wavfile.write("music.wav", SR, (mix * 32767).astype(np.int16))   # numbers -> sound file
Shell · glue sound to video
ffmpeg -framerate 30 -i frames/%04d.jpg -i music.wav \
       -af loudnorm=I=-14 -c:v libx264 -c:a aac launch.mp4

The chain of questions

  1. We want music that hits each moment of the video. A downloaded song or a recording cannot do that from code.
  2. What is sound? A long list of numbers that moves a speaker.
  3. The simplest list is a smooth wave. It sounds like a beep.
  4. Why? A wooden bar sings many notes at once, and it fades. So we add overtones and a fade.
  5. Add a tiny "tok" for the mallet. Now it is a marimba.
  6. Put notes on a ticking clock, in happy chords, with drums. Now it is music.
  7. Give each sound a time from the video. Now it fits.
